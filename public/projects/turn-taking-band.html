Turn-Taking Band

A Wearable Device Supporting Turn-Takings in Hybrid Settings

Key takeaway: Hybrid turn-taking is not only about deciding who speaks next. Participants also need shared awareness of speaking opportunities, audio breakdowns, and attention across locations.

Overview

Hybrid conversations divide attention across physical rooms, screens, and audio channels. Signals that normally mean “I want to speak” or “your turn” become harder to notice. When the local conversation takes priority, remote participants may remain silent rather than risk interrupting or creating overlapping audio.

Research question

What turn-taking challenges emerge in a hybrid setting, and how might technology support them?

Analysis focus

The analysis examined speaker transitions, silence, subgroup dynamics, communication breakdowns, and differences in participation between the two locations.

Three observed barriers

1. Finding a moment to enter

Remote participants often watched while collocated participants continued talking. Concern about interruption and overlapping audio made silence the safer choice. Verbal invitations alone did not reliably dissolve the two subgroups.

Interpretation: The remote group lacked a clear and socially safe way to claim the conversational floor.

2. Detecting audio breakdowns

Participants could not readily determine whether an audio problem originated from their own microphone or the other site. In one episode, the failure became obvious only when a break was announced, after communication had already been lost.

Interpretation: Hybrid settings make the state of the communication channel less visible, delaying repair.

3. Maintaining shared focus

Fatigue, inconvenient timing, and attention directed toward the local room reduced readiness to collaborate across sites. When one side dominated, the distributed group could disengage from the joint activity.

Interpretation: Attention in a hybrid setting tends to remain local unless the system actively redirects it.

Design concept

The proposed system combines a wearable band with an extension for meeting software. Together, they make speaking requests, queue position, and the current speaker visible across locations.

Interaction flow

Request a turn — A participant taps the band to join a shared queue.

Wait in the queue — The band turns yellow and displays the number of speakers ahead.

Receive the floor — The band turns green and gives one long vibration.

Redirect attention across sites — Participants at the other location receive two short vibrations.

Complete the turn — The speaker taps the band again, and the next person advances.

Maintain shared awareness — The meeting interface highlights the current speaker and displays queue information.

Band states

🔴 Red — not your turn: The participant is outside the active speaking floor.

🟡 Yellow — queued: The participant is waiting; a number shows the queue position.

🟢 Green — speak now: The participant currently holds the floor.

From evidence to design

Unclear entry point

Observed problem: Remote participants did not know when they could safely join.

Proposed response: Shared queue and red/yellow/green states.

Intended effect: Make speaking opportunities explicit.

Audio problems noticed late

Observed problem: Participants could not quickly identify a failed audio handoff.

Proposed response: Allocated turns and cross-site vibration.

Intended effect: Make failed handoffs easier to notice and repair.

Attention stays local

Observed problem: Participants focused on people in the same room.

Proposed response: Cross-site vibration and on-screen speaker highlighting.

Intended effect: Redirect attention toward the active speaker at the other site.

Limitations and open questions

The prototype has not been evaluated in a real-world or controlled study.

The exploratory activity was small and conducted only once.

The recordings did not support reliable analysis of eye gaze.

Wearables may introduce cost, comfort, accessibility, adoption, and training barriers.

Poorly timed alerts could distract or overwhelm participants.

A rigid queue could suppress spontaneous conversation and reduce enjoyment.

The concept may fit structured discussion better than free-form conversation.

Important distinction

The study identified turn-taking barriers and proposed a design response. It did not demonstrate that the Turn-Taking Band improves participation, inclusion, or collaboration.

Proposed next study

Build a functional prototype and compare it with a standard video meeting condition.

Central evaluation question

Can explicit turn coordination improve balanced participation without making conversation feel mechanical?

Conclusion

Turn-taking support should coordinate awareness across sites, not merely assign the next speaker.

The Turn-Taking Band reframes hybrid turn-taking as a shared-awareness problem. Its queue, visual states, vibration patterns, and meeting-interface highlights form a testable design proposal—but its effectiveness remains an open research question.
